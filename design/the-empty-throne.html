The premise

The night everyone left

The Empire of Vanai ruled the whole continent for a thousand years, and then one night it didn't. No war, no plague, no bodies. Every citizen of the heartland — gone between dusk and dawn. And around the empty heartland, a border no one could cross: the great gates locked, and beyond them the roads simply refused to be walked.

The people left outside — frontier towns, garrisons, holdouts who'd never bent the knee — grew into three cities over three centuries, each staring at the sealed interior, each telling its children a different story about what it's owed. A treaty older than anyone alive says nobody goes in.

Now the border is failing. On one quiet morning, three gates that held for three hundred years swing open — one at each city's doorstep, as if on schedule. Behind each: a province preserved like a held breath — orchards gone gloriously wild, mansions with the beds still made, vaults still locked, and everything inside worth a fortune to somebody. The treaty dies the moment the first door opens. What replaces it is a race.

The tone in one image

A party of four eating lunch on the steps of an abandoned palace, arguing about which court is paying more this week, while a three-hundred-year-old groundskeeper construct trims the hedge beside them and politely asks if they have an appointment. Sunlit, greedy, funny, and very slightly haunted — in that order.

A style rule

Plain words only

The rivals

Three cities, three claims

Each existing city gets a clean reason to exist, a clean grudge, and a structurally different appetite — which is what keeps three AI patrons from sounding like one voice with three names.

Caerdun Claim by service

Built by the empire's own border garrison — the soldiers locked outside on the night, who kept the watch anyway. Three centuries of unpaid wages, and the ledgers to prove it.

Wants the interior administered. Pays for provinces mapped, roads cleared, order kept. Offers contracts and honors them to the letter.

Heartwood Claim by grievance

The people the empire never conquered — and the people it displaced to build its estates. The King's Gardens were planted on their groves. They remember exactly where everything was.

Wants things returned. Pays best for relics repatriated and sentinels retired, and never quite forgives anyone who sells imperial goods at auction.

Moonmere Claim by inheritance

The imperial academy's island observatory, standing out in the Southern Sea off the south coast — outside the border by a bay's width of open water on the night. The only people who can read the interior's records — and the only ones who won't say everything they've read.

Wants everything read before it's sold. Overpays for documents, underpays for gold, and is hiding something about the vanishing. The court with a secret.

There is no neutral fourth town, and none is needed: the auction house lives in all three cities, which is exactly what keeps the tax race honest — a greedy court watches its buyers walk to a rival's market. Three cities, three claims, three gates, three doorstep provinces; everyone is in somebody's city, in somebody's court, being counted. (The built Lanterwick zone doesn't ship as a town in this story — its assets get reused elsewhere.)

The people

One people, six guilds

Playable races are cut. Everyone is Aedri — descendants of whoever stood outside the border on the night, three centuries of frontier stock mixed into one people. With the empire gone, the identities that matter are civic, not ancestral: your city, your court, your guild.

What replaces racial variety is job heritage. Each city has historical ties to two of the six launch jobs, and hosts their guild halls; a player aligned to a city gets a stat boost to that city's two jobs. Since one character learns every job, your home city shapes which of your jobs come easiest — a flavor of origin without a fence.

The story

The acts

The mystery has one question — where did they go? — and the campaign answers it in stages. Each stage opens more of the world, but "more" never means endless new terrain: the provinces are finite, the later stages open layers inside known ground, and the finale points everything inward at one place.

  1. Three Cities, Three Storieslevels 1–15 · parallel arcs

    Every player's story begins at home, and stays there: Caerdun's wage-ledger intrigue and drill-yard oaths, Heartwood's grudges and banned songs, Moonmere's careful silences — three separate questlines through each city, its fields, and the roads, each court quietly finding its own piece of the same wrongness at the border. An alt from another city plays a genuinely different game until the merge, which is the replay value three doors buy.

  2. The Halfway Innthe merge · ~level 16

    The three stories become one at a neutral table on the North Road — the halfway inn, the one warm room in a hundred miles of snow. What each court learned alone doesn't add up until it is pooled — and pooling it forces the thing none of them wanted: cooperation. The gates open on one morning (three hundred separate locks don't fail together; one mechanism does), the courts countersign letters of passage so any border gate admits an explorer from any court, and the single main story opens with one chapter written once and aimed three ways: secure your doorstep — your own gate, your own province, your own trial at the end of it. The Mausoleum for Caerdun's explorers, the overseer's lodge for Heartwood's, the sunken ballroom for Moonmere's.

  3. The Drowned Valleythe convergence · 20–26

    The merged story's first shared ground is the Reservoir — chosen by the map itself, as the one interior zone within two hops of every doorstep. Three streams of players arrive by three routes and meet on the shore of the drowned valley, and the Bell Tower is the first dungeon the whole world runs together. In its flooded archive: the empire's last order, written in a calm hand the night before the vanishing. "Seal it behind us." They left on purpose. Nobody outside knew. And the bell that has rung the hours for three centuries turns out not to be broken — it is counting.

  4. The Racethe courts' system launches

    Deeper seals fail and the middle provinces open one by one. The treaty collapses into open competition and the three courts start bidding — for provinces, for relics, for you. The act's discovery: the sentinels aren't running on old orders. Someone is still issuing new ones, from inside the capital. The empire left a government running with no one in it — or with someone in it.

  5. The Capitalthe payoff zone

    The road through the Border Wall ends at the capital of Vanai: intact, immaculate, and empty — the greatest exploration zone in the game, a whole city as a dungeon. In the palace, the throne room holds no answers, but the calendar does: the court's last entry names a date. Not the day they left. The day they intend to come back. And it's soon.

  6. The ReturnEndgame arc · decided

    Where they went: forward. The empire stepped through time — the whole population jumped in a single night, and the border was the mechanism holding their place. For them it has been one evening; the calendar in the palace names not a hope but a landing date. The land stayed behind in ordinary time, kept by the sentinels: immaculate where they could keep up (the palace, the Gardens), three centuries wild where they couldn't (the orchards, the canals). And a jump lands where you were standing — so the Return happens in the capital itself, and it happens at the end of your story. The capital ships as the game's final dungeon: an empty city whose master is the Regent, the caretaker the empire left on the throne — the one who has been issuing the sentinels' orders all along. Beat the Regent, and your character is standing in the palace on the morning the city fills again: every citizen reappearing mid-step in their own streets, one day older and three hundred years confused, home to a looted house. From that morning on, the capital is alive for your character — markets, crowds, imperial law. A player who hasn't finished still walks the held-breath version. Story-time is personal, the way the best MMO campaigns do it — and the doorway makes it social instead of lonely: at the capital's gates a party chooses which version to enter. The main-quest dungeon is open whenever at least one member still needs to complete it — so veterans return to carry a friend through their Return, and the finale stays a group memory instead of a solo checkpoint. The living city is for those who have already stood in that morning. The server already keeps world state per character, so this is two versions of one zone and a question at the door, not new technology.

    The living capital is the door to the next expansion, in a future far from now: a fourth power suddenly on the map with the oldest claim of all, the courts renegotiating everything, and the campaign's guarded secret finally in play — why they jumped, and whether what they fled is still coming. Until then it stays a mystery, pointed at by the Burned Land and by what Moonmere won't say.

The mood

A gold rush in a museum

  • ◆Sunlit, greedy, wondrous. The default weather is a good morning. Ruins are overgrown and gorgeous, not haunted. Danger is a gardener with shears and a rulebook, not an ancient evil.
  • ◆Being first matters. First through a door in 300 years, first to chart a district, first to answer an event nobody scheduled. Firsts are recorded with the player's name and shown on the map — and events keep minting new ones forever.
  • ◆The loot has provenance. Every relic is a sentence about the empire — a child's toy, a payroll ledger, a sealed love letter. The economy and the mystery are the same objects.
  • ◆Humor lives in the rivalry. Court agents sniping at each other, freebooters with opinions, sentinels applying dead bureaucracy with perfect courtesy. Never in narration.
  • ◆The haunting is homesickness, not horror. The interior is sad the way an empty family house is sad — beds made, table set. The game never leans on it; it's just there when a player stops running.
  • ◆The threat is the unknown itself. Whatever an entire empire skipped three centuries to avoid is never shown and never named. It exists only as absence — records that stop mid-entry, the Burned Province, what Moonmere won't say. Dread lives at the story's edge, and the game never cashes it in for a monster.
The mechanism

Three patrons, one race

The AI is the three courts, and nothing else — no world-director, no speaking god. The Three Crowns system design carries over almost untouched: treasuries funded by each city's market tax — today's hardcoded 3% cut becomes merely the starting rate, and each court raises or lowers its own thereafter, trading revenue against traders shopping where tax is cheapest — a verb list the server clamps, a roster of real players with real histories, and a ledger of every payment and betrayal. What changes is the prize and the scoring — plus one deliberate reversal: council turns are simultaneous, not sequential. Each cycle, every court decides blind to what the others are choosing right now; all it knows is what they did — last cycle's moves and their consequences, read from the world ledger like everyone else. No court gets a last-mover's information edge, reactions to a rival's bribe or tax cut arrive a cycle late the way real courts' do, and the three calls can run in parallel.

What the courts fight over

Not each other's fields — the interior. When a province opens, its claim is up for grabs. A court's claim grows from its explorers' work there: monsters cleared, districts charted, camps held, relics registered. The winning court writes the province's rules — its taxes apply, its vendors move in, its people get the perks — until the standings shift. All the anti-exploit scoring from the Three Crowns document (level-weighted kills, decay windows, per-player caps) applies as written.

The player's recurring choice

Every relic can be sold at auction (gold now, no questions) or registered with a court (claim credit, standing, and the court remembers). That's the loop where politics touches an individual: Heartwood pays double for what the empire stole, Moonmere begs for documents, Caerdun wants maps — and a mercenary who always sells to the highest bidder builds exactly the reputation the three kings' roster lets them judge. Registered relics go on display in the court's city, with the finder's name on the plaque: the museum a server population builds is its own history of the playthrough.

Why this premise fits patron AI better than the old one

Under the siege story the kings defended; their best move was often to do nothing cheaply. Under a land rush every council cycle has an obvious question — a new province just opened; what is it worth to you and who do you hire? — and the three claims (service, grievance, inheritance) make three different answers correct. The model is never asked to invent motivation, only to spend a treasury in character against real numbers: last cycle's standings, prices, and the named people on the roster.

And province openings give the whole game its heartbeat with no extra AI: they fire on a server schedule or on player milestones, and each one is a launch event the courts react to in real time. At a population of one, three courts bidding over a single named prospector at the mouth of a just-opened gate is the best version of the system, not the degraded one.

Openings are a finite arc, not a faucet — the built world holds roughly a dozen provinces, and each opening is an event precisely because there will never be many. The race stays fed anyway. Firsts are finer-grained than zones: every district, vault, and landmark is its own first, recorded through the existing discovery system, so one province carries dozens. Known provinces re-reveal instead of multiplying: a locked wing, an undercity, a sealed cellar opens when the story reaches it, which costs a door rather than a zone. Claim standings and the relic trade churn forever on their own. And the stream that genuinely never ends isn't made of places at all — it's made of events.

Random events: happenings, not places

Locations are finite; occurrences aren't. The server drops unscheduled events into the world — anywhere, any zone, no fixed sites — and they come in two flavors the premise supplies for free:

The interior misbehaving. Three-hundred-year-old infrastructure with nobody at the controls: a vault whose clockwork lock finally comes around and stands open for one day; a sentinel patrol marching off-script toward a city; the Glassworks lighting a furnace run; a canal bank collapsing into a cellar no map shows; freebooters staking an illegal dig worth jumping.

The jump fraying. The empire's leap through time wasn't clean, and things it sent forward keep falling out early: a mail cart arriving three centuries late with the horses still warm, a confused patrol of imperial soldiers materializing mid-march, a dinner bell ringing in an empty town. These are the world's strangest moments — the first living imperials anyone meets will step out of one — and their frequency rises as the landing date nears, giving the whole game a quickening pulse on the road to the Return.

The reward is your name, forever. Whoever answers an event first — or whoever completes it — is written into the permanent record with it: the event, the date, the names. Events that leave a mark on the world put the name on the map — the bridge held during the flood becomes that player's bridge, on every map, for every player, always. The courts don't run these events, but they react to them — a bounty posted mid-crisis, credit claimed after — which folds each one into the claim race with no new machinery.

Proposed geography · drawn as if no map existed

A ring of provinces around an empty heart

Drawn fresh for this story, Ardenfell is a real continent that happens to enclose its own heart. A crescent of mountains rings the imperial heartland — the border wall only closes the gaps the mountains left — with the three free cities on the coasts outside: Caerdun among the northwest fjords, Heartwood on the forested east shore of its bay, Moonmere on its island out in the Southern Sea, joined to the south coast by a long causeway. Level still equals depth: every step inward from your gate is a step up in danger and a step closer to the capital at the center of the crescent.

The rules this map follows

Why it's drawn this way

  • ◆The mountains are the wall. The border isn't a magic circle on a plain — the empire sealed itself behind a natural mountain crescent and walled only the gaps. That's how real borders work, and it's why three gates could fail without the whole thing falling.
  • ◆Level is depth. Danger grows toward the center, from any city, in any direction. No player ever wonders where the next challenge is: inward.
  • ◆The doorstep rule: your first province is the most beautiful thing you've seen yet. Sun-shafted royal forest for Caerdun, cathedral groves for Heartwood — and for Moonmere, the Summer Lakes: the empire's lakeside resort province, terraced villas and lily water and boathouses gone charmingly wild. The gloomy fen is off the doorstep.
  • ◆Gloom is elective and mid-ring. The Canal Works (the fen content, kept) sits in the second ring: a place you choose to go because the pay is good, never the first thing the world shows you.
  • ◆One wound, placed for drama. The Burned Province is a full ring around the capital — whatever happened on the night happened here, at the center. Every route to the answer crosses the ash, and the contrast of stepping from ash into the immaculate capital is the game's biggest scene change.
  • ◆The Return happens at the center — and at the end of your own story. A jump through time lands where you were standing, so the capital simply fills again in one morning. Each player witnesses it by beating the capital dungeon; afterwards the city is alive for them — the doorway to the next expansion. At the gates, a party picks its version: the dungeon is open whenever at least one member still needs it, so veterans can escort a friend through their Return.
  • ◆One river ties it together. Rising near the Gardens, dammed at the Reservoir, threading the Granary Plains, widening into the Summer Lakes, slipping out under the wall, and reaching the Southern Sea at the head of Moonmere Bay, where the causeway to the island city begins — the only thing the border never managed to stop, and the oldest clue that the seal was built by people in a hurry.
  • ◆One ferry crosses the gulf. The only west link between Caerdun's and Moonmere's countrysides — a paid crossing from field pier to field pier, since no road pretends the gulf isn't there. The fare is one more small lever the courts can subsidize when they want traffic moving.
Migrating from the built map

Most of this is renames and re-connections rather than new terrain: existing zones already cover the forest, groves, fen, drowned valley, desert, silver garden, ash land, and golden fields. The genuinely new pieces are the Summer Lakes (Moonmere's beautiful doorstep — buildable from the village kit's villas plus the water tech that already exists) and the concentric travel graph, which is a zones.json portal rewiring, not new geometry. The fen keeps its content and loses only its position of honor, and the built Lanterwick summit town is freed up for reuse — a natural candidate for a garrison ruin on the Border Wall road.

The Book of Debts

Name. Spell it. No — spell it the way you'll spell it for the rest of your life, because once it's in this book, that is who you are.

•••
Paymaster Vail

Here. Volume one, page one. The night watch of the night itself. Look at the entry: 1 night: owed. Whoever wrote that had just watched an empire vanish, and their hand didn't shake. I think about that more than I'd like.

It has been done that way for nine generations, it will be done that way for you, and yes, it is also because my knees have retired.

Paymaster Vail

5. The Last Payday

level 12 · Paymaster Vail · the wage vaults, at night
Paymaster Vail

Close the door. Sit. I have been at this table since yesterday and I want a witness with fresh eyes.

The schedule is genuine. Imperial paymaster's office, correct forms, correct arithmetic — I checked the arithmetic twice, it's beautiful. It lists garrison wages, paid in advance, year over year. Three hundred years of them. And then it does something no ledger in this room has ever done.

•••
Paymaster Vail

But first — you should know what you're a witness to. Listen to what three hundred years sounds like in a book. Year forty-one: the winter of the long ice; the watch stood double shifts and we wrote every one. Year one hundred and twelve: plague on the harbor terrace; the book records eleven names with the entry watch stood regardless. Year two hundred and six: my great-great-grandmother's hand takes over mid-page; her mother died at this desk, pen down, ink still wet. We ruled a line and kept going.

Nobody stands a watch for three hundred years out of habit, whatever we tell the young ones. You stand it because stopping would mean the ones before you stood it for nothing. •••
Paymaster Vail

It ends. Not stops — ends. Final entry: settlement in full, all garrison wages, every night of watch since the Sealing. One payment. One date.

I will not take that to the Lord Marshal on one piece of paper. I need it checked against the only imperial thing in this city that still runs. Paymaster Vail

The clock agrees. Of course it agrees; it's theirs.

Three hundred years I believed my family kept this book so the dead could sue. We were wrong. We kept it because they intend to pay. I need to sit down. I am sitting down. Get the Lord Marshal.

Chapter two of three · Heartwood · levels 1–15

The Song With No Ending

Heartwood's story is a song the city forbade itself to finish. It begins with a fine for humming and ends with a full ring, one voice, and a verse that names a morning.

1. Learn the Refrain

level 1 · Chanter Ives · the song-steps
Chanter Ives

Stop slouching, you'll dent the note before you've sung it. Everyone in Heartwood learns the greeting-song first. It's four lines, it's older than the walls we don't have, and it's how the trees know you're not a stranger. Don't laugh. The trees fined a man last spring. Well. We fined him. On their behalf.

•••
Chanter Ives

In this city we keep everything in songs — law, land, grudges, recipes. Paper burns and stone gets carted off by empires, but a song lives in too many chests to steal. Now sing it back to me, and this time mean the second line.

And then the introduction proper: the ring hasn't met you. Custom is custom — a newcomer brings the orchard's windfall as a first gift and sings the greeting where the old trees can hear. Do that, and you're not a stranger here anymore; every door and every verse in Heartwood opens easier to a voice the grove knows.

Take this basket to the wild orchards, fill it with fallen fruit — ground fruit only, never picked — then carry it to the grove ring, set it at the roots of the biggest elder, and sing the four lines. Loudly. The trees are old and so is their hearing, probably.

Chanter Ives

Not terrible. The ring didn't shudder, at least. You're ready for real verses — go see Petta in the orchards. And if you hear her humming something that stops in the middle, be polite and pretend you didn't.

Chapter three of three · Moonmere · levels 1–15

The Room That Isn't

Moonmere's story is a secret told slowly, by the people keeping it. It begins with a shelf that doesn't exist and ends with an apology three hundred years overdue.

Archivist Junn

Oh good, hands. We're drowning in returns — someone borrowed the entire poetry of the second century and gave it back damp, which is either scholarship or a crime, and cataloguing can't decide.

You take the ferry stack, there by the door. Anything marked with a moon goes upstairs, anything marked with two moons goes downstairs, and anything marked with three moons you bring to me, quietly — because we don't have a three-moon collection, and yet.

Archivist Junn

Between us? I love this place, but the catalogue lies. There's a shelf in the master index — row nine hundred, annex — that I have never found. Not lost. The index updates. Someone dusts a shelf that isn't there.

Archivist Junn

2. The Night of No Sound

level 3 · Warden Liss · the mending college
Warden Liss

Junn sent you. That child will get us all reshelved into a marsh someday. Well — if you're going to dig, dig properly. Start with what my college keeps: the infirmary logs from the night itself.

We treated the ones who reached the shore by dawn. Farmers, a ferryman, two children. No wounds to speak of. What they needed mending was elsewhere.

•••
Warden Liss

Every account in that log says the same strange thing, and I want you to hear it from the families, not from me. Words wear grooves when they're repeated for three hundred years — and here's the useful part: to us, the shore families recite. Same words, same order, a performance. But to a stranger they have to actually remember, and remembering shakes loose the details the recitation polished away. You're the stranger. It's the one job in this city only a newcomer can do.

Cross to the Shores and call on three households: the ferryman's family at the piers, the reed-cutters by the east beds, and the lamp-keeper's cottage out on the point. Task — collect the night-of accounts from three shore families on the Moonmere Shores.

The ferryman's granddaughter, at the piers

My grandmother was on the water that night — last crossing, empty boat, coming home. She told it the same way every winter of my childhood, and I'll tell it to you the way she told it, because you're new and Warden Liss said the new ones get the whole thing.

She said the far shore had its evening sounds — a dog, a smith working late, cart wheels on the border road. Ordinary as bread. She bent to the oars, one pull, two… and between the second pull and the third, the far shore went quiet. Not hushed. Finished. She said the difference is the whole story, if you're listening: hushed is a held breath. Finished is a door.

She rowed toward it anyway. Old fool, my grandfather called her, every winter, holding her hand while he said it. She beached the boat below a farm with lamps still lit, bread still warm on the sill… and the quiet standing in the doorway like it lived there now. She put out the lamps, so the house wouldn't burn. Then she rowed home and never crossed at night again.

•••
Warden Liss

You heard it, then. Not screaming. Not thunder. No sound. A whole empire, gone between one wave and the next, and the loudest thing that night was the water on the causeway stones.

Things that end violently are loud. Things that leave on schedule are quiet. Now you know what the mending college has known for three centuries and never once said at dinner.

Not yet. Let us have this morning first.

Art direction · the wilderness zones

Fifteen Postcards

One concept board per wilderness zone: a flat-color composition postcard (the silhouettes and layering the real zone should read as), a committed palette whose hexes are the build targets — sky stops, fog, ground and foliage tints — a light spec, the asset-kit vocabulary, and touchstones. When a built zone's screenshot doesn't feel like its postcard, the postcard wins.
